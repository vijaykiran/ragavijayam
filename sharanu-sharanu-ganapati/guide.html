Sharanu Sharanu Ganapati — Complete Learning Guide

Raga: Hamsadhwani  |  Tala: Adi tala in tishra gati  |  Language: Kannada (with Sanskrit epithets)  |  Deity: Ganapati Your sruti: Male range — this guide uses Sa = A throughout (your notes list G#, A, A#; A is the middle option, adjust half a step either way if your voice prefers).

1. The Raga: Hamsadhwani (“the sound of the swan”)

Hamsadhwani is one of the most beloved audava (5-note) ragas, created by Ramaswami Dikshitar (father of Muthuswami Dikshitar). It is bright, auspicious, and traditionally sung at the start of a concert — very fitting for a Ganapati song, since Ganapati is invoked first (prathama smarana, as the charana itself says!).

Swaras At your sruti (Sa = A)
Arohana (ascent) S R2 G3 P N3 Ṡ A – B – C# – E – G# – A
Avarohana (descent) Ṡ N3 P G3 R2 S A – G# – E – C# – B – A

Key things to internalize:

  • No Ma, no Dha. The jumps G→P (skipping Ma) and P→N (skipping Dha) are the sound of Hamsadhwani. If you accidentally sing Ma or Dha, it stops being Hamsadhwani.
  • N3 (kakali nishada) is just one semitone below Sa. The N→Ṡ resolution is the raga’s signature “lift”. Practice landing N precisely — it should feel like it’s leaning into Sa.
  • Jeeva swaras (life-giving notes): G and N. Phrases love to rest on P and G.
  • Famous reference kritis you can listen to for the raga’s flavour: Vatapi Ganapatim (Dikshitar), Raghunayaka (Tyagaraja), Jagadanandakaraka is not Hamsadhwani — a common mix-up; it’s Nattai. Listening to Vatapi Ganapatim is the single best way to get this raga in your ear.

Note on one line in your notes: the lines “ṣṛg̣ṃ p̣ ḍ ṇ ṣ / ṡ ṙ ġ ṁ ṗ ḋ ṅ ṡ” contain Ma and Dha, which do not exist in Hamsadhwani. I checked the teacher’s full recording — nothing like this is sung anywhere in it. It’s a stray scale-reference line (or transcription artifact), and it’s excluded from the corrected notation below.

2. The Tala: Adi tala in tishra gati

Adi tala has 8 beats per cycle (avartana), counted with the hands as:

Beat 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
Action Clap little finger ring finger middle finger Clap wave Clap wave
Anga Laghu Drutam Drutam

Tishra gati means each beat is subdivided into 3 pulses instead of the usual 4. So one avartana = 8 × 3 = 24 pulses, and the music has a lilting, waltz-like swing: ta-ki-ta, ta-ki-ta…

How to practice the tala before adding melody:

  1. Put on the metronome track I made (tishra_adi_tala.wav). The high tick = claps (beats 1, 5, 7), the low ticks = finger counts and waves.
  2. Say “ta-ki-ta” on every beat while clapping the Adi pattern.
  3. Once comfortable, hum a single Sa in the tishra rhythm against the claps.

3. Reading the Notation

Your notes use standard conventions:

  • Plain lowercase letters (s r g p n) = madhya sthayi (middle octave).
  • Dot above (ṡ ṙ ġ) = tara sthayi (upper octave).
  • Dot below (ṣ ṇ p̣) = mandra sthayi (lower octave).
  • Comma ( , ) = extend the previous note by one pulse.
  • Letters joined together (e.g. rpgr, snnṙ) = fast cluster — those notes share the time of roughly one or two pulses.

4. Pallavi — line by line

The pallavi is the refrain you return to after every section. Learn it until it’s automatic.

Line 1 — “Sharanu sharanu Ganapati”

ṡ   n   ṡ   n   n   p    p   p   p   g  ,
Sha-ra -nu  sha-ra -nu    Ga - na- pa - ti

(Corrected: the written “s” is actually tara ṡ — the song opens on the upper Sa.)

Meaning: Sharanu (Kannada, from Sanskrit śaraṇa) = “I take refuge in you / salutations”. So: “Refuge, refuge in you, O Ganapati.”

How it moves: opens boldly on the upper Sa, rocks down to Ni (ṡ–n–ṡ–n), settles onto Pa for “nu…Ga-na-pa”, then relaxes down to G on “ti”. At your sruti: it starts on the upper A, and the phrase settles through G# (Ni) and E (Pa) down to C# (Ga).

Line 2 — “Sharanu sharanu Ganapati” (second time, different tune)

g   g   r   g  (n p)   r   r   r   s  ,
Sha-ra -nu  sha-ra-nu   Ga - na- pa - ti

(All middle octave, as written. In the recording the “n p” of the second “sharanu” is a quick up-and-down arc — the voice sweeps from G up through N and falls back to P in one graceful gesture, not two separate held notes. The teacher also touches a tiny mandra ṇ grace just before the final s.)

How it moves: answers line 1 from below — G G R, the n–p arc, then R R R resolving to Sa: the “coming home” feeling. Lines 1+2 together are sung twice (the ×2 in your notes).

Line 3 — “Mahakaaya mahaveerya maha mahima Ganapati”

g p , n ,  n    p n ,  ṙ , ṙ     ṡ ṡ ,  n p   n  ṡ ṡ ṡ ṡ , ,
Ma-ha-kaa-ya    ma-ha-vee-rya    ma-ha  ma-hi-ma Ga-na-pa-ti

(Corrected: the written “r , r” is tara ṙ — confirmed in the recording at ~14–16s.)

Meaning: Mahākāya = of mighty body; mahāvīrya = of great valor/power; mahā mahima = of great glory. This line climbs into the upper register — the melodic high point of the pallavi, matching the “greatness” the words describe. The shape is a steady climb: g–p–n, then p–n–ṙ, then the ṡ plateau.

Line 4 — “Mahateja mahadeva nandanashree Ganapati”

p n ,  ṙ , ṙ    p n ,  ṙ , ṙ    ṙ ġ ṙ  ṙpġṙ   ṡ n n ṙ
Ma-ha-te-ja     ma-ha-de-va     nan-da-na shree Ga-na-pa-ti

(Corrected: all the r’s and g’s in this line are tara ṙ/ġ. “shree” takes the ṙpġṙ figure, in which the first pair ṙp is underlined in class notation — the two swaras share one pulse, i.e. double speed — then ġ and ṙ get a pulse each.)

Meaning: Mahāteja = of great radiance; Mahādeva nandana = beloved son of Mahadeva (Shiva); shree = auspicious. The repeated p n , ṙ , ṙ motif makes this line easy to memorize — same cell twice, then the ornamented cadence ṡ n n ṙ that ends most lines of this song.

Line 5 — “Eka danta neka charita shree kara Ganapati”

ṙ ġ ṙ  ġ,ġ    ṙġṙ    ġ ġ ġ     ṙ ġ ṙ     ṡnp   ṡ n n    ṙ ,
E-ka   dan-ta ne-ka  cha-ri-ta shree-ka  ra    Ga-na-pa ti

(Corrected: every “g” in this line is tara ġ. Word alignment per your class: “shree-ka” rides ṙ–ġ–ṙ, “ra” takes the fast ṡnp cascade, “Ga-na-pa” is ṡ–n–n, and “ti” lands on ṙ. In the recording this line is sung twice.)

Meaning: Ekadanta = the one-tusked; (a)neka charita = of countless deeds (danta + aneka merge in pronunciation to “dantāneka”); shreekara = bestower of auspiciousness. This is the highest-sitting line of the song — it rocks between tara ṙ and ġ before cascading down ṡ–n–p.

Line 6 — “Paaka shaasanaadi vandya shoka doora Ganapati”

ṡ , ṡ   ġ ṙ ġ   p, p   ṡ n ṙ    ṙ ġ ṙ   ṙpġṙ   ṡ n n ṙ
Paa-ka  shaa-sa-naa-di van-dya  sho-ka-doo ra   Ga-na-pa-ti

(Corrected: “s , s” is tara ṡ and the g’s are tara ġ — but note “p, p” for “naa-di” really does drop to the middle-octave P, a lovely momentary dip (heard at ~38.7s, held ¾ of a second). Then it climbs right back up.)

Meaning: Pākaśāsana = “chastiser of the demon Pāka” = Indra; ādi vandya = worshipped by (Indra) and all the gods; śoka dūra = who keeps sorrow far away. Then return to Sharanu sharanu Ganapati ×2 to close the pallavi.

5. Anupallavi — line by line

“Shoorpa karna taapa harana sarpaabharana Ganapati” (×2)

g p g    p , p    g p g    p p p    p p ,    p p g    p p ṡ n ,
Shoor-pa kar-na   taa-pa   ha-ra-na sar-paa  bha-ra-na Ga-na-pa-ti

(Corrected: the “s” near the end is tara ṡ — the line rocks gently around g–p, then “Ganapati” lifts p–p–ṡ–n.)

Meaning: Śūrpakarṇa = whose ears are like winnowing fans; tāpa haraṇa = remover of suffering/afflictions; sarpābharaṇa = adorned with serpents. Notice the melody hovers around G–P — a gentle rocking that contrasts with the pallavi.

“Paapa harana suprasanna prema poorna Ganapati” (×2)

p , p    ṡ n ṙ    p , p    ṡ, n ṙ    ṙ ġ ṙ    ṙpġṙ    ṡ n n ṙ
Paa-pa   ha-ra-na su-pra   san-na    pre-ma   poor-na  Ga-na-pa-ti

(Corrected: “s n r” both times is ṡ n ṙ — up to tara Sa, lean on n, land on tara ṙ. The cadence figure is the same ṙpġṙ as in the pallavi — ṙp underlined, two swaras in one pulse; the p is the middle-octave pa, a quick passing dip.)

Meaning: Pāpa haraṇa = destroyer of sins; suprasanna = serenely gracious; prema pūrṇa = full of love. This line climbs to the tara register (ṙ ġ ṙ) — the emotional peak of the anupallavi.

“Naama roopa rahita hema varna deva Ganapati” (×2)

ṙ ġ ṙ    ġ , ġ    ṙ ġ ṙ    ġ, ġ    ṙ ġ ṙ    ṡ n p    ṡ n n  ṙ ,
Naa-ma   roo-pa   ra-hi-ta he-ma   var-na   de-va    Ga-na-pa-ti

(Corrected: every “g” is tara ġ and “s n p” is ṡ n p — same high territory as the Eka danta line of the pallavi.)

Meaning: Nāma rūpa rahita = beyond all name and form (the formless absolute); hema varṇa = golden-hued; a lovely philosophical paradox — formless, yet golden.

“Vimala bharita sumana charita namo swami Ganapati” (×2)

s s s    g r g    p p p    ṡ n ṙ    |    ṙ ġ ṙ    ṙpġṙ    ṡ n n ṙ
Vi-ma-la bha-ri-ta su-ma-na cha-ri-ta   na-mo    swa-mi   Ga-na-pa-ti

(Corrected: the line starts in the middle octave exactly as written — s s s, g r g, p p p — then “cha-ri-ta” vaults up: ṡ n ṙ, and “namo swami Ganapati” stays tara: ṙ ġ ṙ. This one line spans the song’s whole range — a beautiful summary of the raga.)

Meaning: Vimala bharita = filled with purity; sumana charita = of beautiful, noble deeds; namo swami = salutations, O Lord. Then: Sharanu sharanu… refrain.

6. Charana — line by line

Good news: the charana reuses the anupallavi’s tunes almost exactly — I verified this in the teacher’s recording (the charana, from ~1:46, tracks the anupallavi’s melodic contours line for line). Once the anupallavi is solid, the charana is mostly new words on known melody. Line-to-line mapping:

Charana line Same tune as anupallavi line
Parama purusha trijaga namita / Sharanu sharanu Ganapati Shoorpa karna…
Parameshwara hridaya vaasa / varada hastha Ganapati Paapa harana…
Prathama smarana paapa harana / sankashta harana Ganapati Naama roopa…
Vidya dharaka bhakta paalaka / namo namo Ganapati Vimala bharita…

Meanings:

  • Parama puruṣa = Supreme Being; trijaga namita = bowed to by all three worlds.
  • Parameśvara hṛdaya vāsa = who dwells in Shiva’s heart; varada hasta = whose hand grants boons.
  • Prathama smaraṇa = the first to be remembered/invoked (before any undertaking); paapa harana = destroyer of sins; saṅkaṭa/sankaṣṭa haraṇa = remover of dire troubles.
  • Vidyā dhāraka = bestower of knowledge; bhakta pālaka = protector of devotees; namo namo = salutations again and again.

The song closes with “(Ganapati) ×4” — typically sung as a joyous, accelerating repetition — then the final Sharanu refrain.

8. Two-Week Practice Plan (20–30 min/day)

Every session starts with: drone on → 2 min humming Sa → sing the scale up/down 5× → 1 min tala clapping with “takita”.

Day Focus
1 Foundation only: drone, scale, tala. Listen to the full song (class recording) 3× without singing. Listen to Vatapi Ganapatim once to absorb Hamsadhwani.
2 Pallavi line 1: swaras first (s ṇ s ṇ ṇ p̣…), then words. 10+ repetitions with the audio.
3 Pallavi line 2. Then join lines 1+2 with the loop track.
4 Lines 1+2 with tala — clap Adi while singing. Slow is fine; never sacrifice the beat.
5 Pallavi lines 3–4 (Mahakaaya / Mahateja). The upper-register climb — don’t strain; support from the breath.
6 Pallavi lines 5–6 (Eka danta / Paaka shaasana). The fast clusters (rpgr, ṡnṙṡ) — sing them slowly first, as plain even notes, then compress.
7 Full pallavi, start to finish, with tala. Record yourself on your phone and compare with the class recording.
8 Anupallavi line 1 (Shoorpa karna) — swaras, then words.
9 Anupallavi line 2 (Paapa harana). Mind the jump to ṙ ġ ṙ.
10 Anupallavi lines 3–4 (Naama roopa / Vimala bharita). Then full anupallavi.
11 Pallavi + anupallavi together, with tala.
12 Charana — mostly known tunes, focus on the new words. Say each line rhythmically as pure text first, then sing.
13 Full song, slow, with tala. Record yourself again.
14 Full song at performance tempo. Compare recordings from day 7 vs day 14. Celebrate 🙂 then sing it once more purely as devotion, not practice.

Golden rules:

  1. Words follow tune, tune follows swaras. Never learn lyrics-first; you’ll bake in wrong notes.
  2. The drone is not optional. Pitch accuracy in Carnatic music comes from constant reference to Sa.
  3. When a phrase won’t stick, sing it 5× on just “aa” — removes the word-processing load.
  4. Meaning fuels expression: you now know every word — sing “shoka doora” like you mean the sorrow leaving.

9. About the song

This is a Kannada devotional song (devaranama/bhajan style) to Ganapati. It follows the classic kriti structure — pallavi, anupallavi, charana — and packs in many of Ganapati’s traditional epithets (Ekadanta, Shoorpakarna, Prathama-smarana). I couldn’t confirm a canonical composer from published sources — it appears to come from the bhajan/devaranama tradition rather than the core classical canon. If your teacher mentioned the composer, it’s worth noting down.
